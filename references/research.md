# Shape the explanation before researching

Read this guide only when the task requires substantial new research or synthesis. If the conversation already contains enough evidence, reuse it. A narrow factual gap calls for a targeted check, not this whole workflow.

## Set a small, revisable understanding brief

Identify four things within the normal planning step:

- **Reader outcome:** What should the reader be able to explain, distinguish, or decide?
- **Question sequence:** Which questions need answering, and in what order will their answers make sense?
- **Evidence needs:** Which facts, comparable examples, definitions, and limitations would answer those questions?
- **Explanatory relationship:** Is the key idea a mechanism, a contrast, a dependency, a change over time, or a distribution?

Use a few sentences, not a separate deliverable or a fixed schema. If a progress message is useful, summarize the intended focus briefly in the user's language. Do not expose internal reasoning or stop for approval unless an important ambiguity or authorization boundary requires it.

Treat the brief as a set of questions, not a promised conclusion or a page blueprint. Do not choose chart counts, column counts, or visual effects yet. Revise the brief when the evidence challenges it; never manufacture missing data to preserve an early design.

## Gather evidence that serves the questions

Use the host's research capabilities and any appropriate domain guidance. VibeView shapes the explanation; it does not replace research methods, domain expertise, or source verification.

Reuse verified material already in context. Seek comparable evidence: the same task, period, population, unit, or definition where the comparison depends on it. Distinguish observed results, a source's claims, assumptions, and recommendations. Keep sources attached to the claims they support so the final page does not require a second source hunt.

When evidence conflicts, show the scope or uncertainty rather than silently selecting the tidiest narrative. A missing data point may call for a qualified explanation or a different visual, not more searches. Follow any research scope or coverage requirement set by the user.

Stop when the main questions can be answered with adequate support and material limitations are clear. Do not widen the research just to fill a layout. If a critical source is unavailable, disclose the gap and its effect on the conclusion; ask a focused question only when proceeding would materially mislead the reader.

## Write the final page from the evidence

Synthesize while composing. Do not generate a full prose report, a separate page specification, and then a third version in HTML. Keep only the working notes the research actually needs. If another agent has already supplied a useful analysis, preserve its relevant evidence instead of repeating its research.

Choose the visual after the evidence is clear enough to support it. Let the reading order answer the reader's questions; it need not mirror the order in which sources were found. Carry qualifications, sources, and important details into the page rather than sacrificing them for a simpler graphic.

## Examples of useful briefs

**A comparison:** For a request to research two integration approaches, determine what each does, how they can work together, and when either is useful. Look for the same concrete task under both approaches and identify the handoffs. A shared workflow may explain more than two lists of features.

**A mechanism:** For a request to explain a payment retry system, identify what triggers a retry, what prevents a duplicate charge, and how failure is reported. Seek the state transitions and at least one exception. Draw a causal sequence only where the evidence supports the connection.

**A decision:** For a request to assess an adoption strategy, establish the decision, constraints, and alternatives before collecting benefits. Seek evidence that could change the recommendation, including switching costs and failure cases. Do not promise a winner before the trade-offs are known.

These are examples, not templates. Use the same principles for other subjects and languages without translating the examples into a mandatory outline.
